Introduction

The Riemann approach partitions the domain into small subintervals and approximates the integral using rectangles.

Riemann Sum
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x \]

The Lebesgue approach partitions the range of function values and considers the measures of their preimages.

Lebesgue Partition
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{j=1}^{M} y_j\,\mu(A_j) \] \[ A_j = \{x\in[a,b] : f(x)\in I_j\} \]

Here Aj is the set of domain points whose function values belong to the j-th range interval.

Comparison of the approaches

Riemann approach

The Riemann method is intuitive and straightforward to implement. Its accuracy depends on how finely the domain is partitioned and on the sampling points selected.

For continuous functions, midpoint sums converge to the integral as the partition becomes finer. Piecewise continuous functions can also be handled effectively.

Lebesgue-inspired approach

The range is divided into intervals, and the algorithm estimates the measure of each preimage using the sampled domain points.

This method can group together points that have similar function values, even if they are far apart in the domain. Its accuracy depends on both domain sampling and range quantization.

Accuracy and efficiency

With N domain samples and K range intervals, both implementations require approximately O(N + K) time and O(K) additional memory for the range histogram. The Riemann method needs only O(1) additional memory if the sum is accumulated directly.

Pathological functions

Dirichlet function

The Dirichlet function equals 1 at rational points and 0 at irrational points.

Dirichlet Function
\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} 1, & x\in\mathbb{Q},\\ 0, & x\notin\mathbb{Q}. \end{cases} \]

It is not Riemann-integrable because its upper and lower sums remain 1 and 0, respectively, for every partition. However, its Lebesgue integral is zero because the rationals have measure zero.

Important numerical limitation: every midpoint generated by this implementation is rational. Therefore, ordinary midpoint sampling returns 1 instead of the Lebesgue integral 0. The range histogram also receives only sampled rational values and cannot detect the irrational points.

Thomae's function

Thomae's function equals 1/q at rational numbers p/q in lowest terms, and zero at irrational numbers.

Thomae's Function
\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{q}, & x=\dfrac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}, \quad \gcd(p,q)=1,\ q>0,\\[6pt] 0, & x\notin\mathbb{Q}. \end{cases} \]

It is Riemann-integrable, with integral zero. Although it is discontinuous at every rational point, its values become small for rationals with large denominators. Consequently, numerical estimates can approach zero as the sampling resolution increases.

Conclusions

The experiments show that the Riemann and Lebesgue perspectives provide two different ways of understanding integration. The Riemann approach focuses on partitioning the domain, while the Lebesgue approach focuses on partitioning the range and measuring the corresponding preimages.

For the continuous and piecewise continuous functions considered here, both numerical methods should converge to the same integral as their discretizations are refined. Any remaining difference is due to numerical approximation, finite sampling, and range quantization.

The singular function 1/√x illustrates that an unbounded function can still have a finite integral. A uniform midpoint grid avoids evaluating the singular endpoint, but convergence may be slower than for bounded smooth functions.

The pathological examples reveal a deeper limitation. Numerical sampling cannot always reproduce the measure-theoretic behavior of a function. The Dirichlet function is particularly important: its Lebesgue integral is zero, but rational midpoint sampling gives one. Thomae's function, on the other hand, is Riemann-integrable despite its discontinuities at all rational points.

Therefore, numerical results must always be interpreted in light of the mathematical properties of the function. Increasing the number of samples improves many approximations, but it does not guarantee that every pathological function will be integrated correctly.

Standard Normal Cumulative Distribution Function

In this second part, we develop and compare numerical methods for computing the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution.

Definition of the normal CDF

The standard normal distribution has mean 0 and variance 1. Its probability density function (PDF) is:

Standard Normal PDF
\[ \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} \]

The cumulative distribution function gives the probability that a standard normal random variable is less than or equal to x:

Standard Normal CDF
\[ \Phi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{t^2}{2}}\,dt \]

Since the total area under the normal density is 1 and the distribution is symmetric around zero, we have Φ(0) = 0.5 and Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x).

Numerical approaches

Method A: Numerical integration

The first method approximates the integral of the probability density function using the composite trapezoidal rule. By symmetry, it is sufficient to integrate from 0 to |x| and then add or subtract the result from 0.5.

Composite Trapezoidal Rule
\[ \int_a^b f(t)\,dt \approx h\left[ \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} +\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}f(a+ih) \right] \] \[ h=\frac{b-a}{N} \]

The interval is divided into N equally spaced subintervals. The area under the density is approximated by trapezoids.

Method B: Power series expansion

The error function has the following power series:

Power Series Expansion
\[ \operatorname{erf}(z) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^{2n+1}} {n!(2n+1)} \]

Substituting z = x/√2 gives a series for the normal CDF. The series converges for every real x, although for large absolute values of x, cancellation can reduce numerical accuracy.

Method C: Error function approximation

The normal CDF can be represented exactly using the error function:

CDF and Error Function
\[ \Phi(x)= \frac{1}{2} \left[ 1+\operatorname{erf} \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \right] \]

Since JavaScript does not provide a native erf function, we implement a fast polynomial approximation. It avoids numerical integration and is particularly efficient for repeated evaluations.

Why is the normal CDF not an elementary function?

Elementary functions include polynomials, rational functions, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions, and combinations of these functions.

The integral of the Gaussian density involves the exponential function e−x². Unlike many familiar functions, this exponential does not have an elementary antiderivative.

This fact follows from Liouville's theorem on elementary integration. In the special case of an integrand eg(x), where g is a polynomial, an elementary antiderivative would require a rational function R(x) satisfying:

\[ R'(x)+g'(x)R(x)=1 \]

For g(x) = −x², this would require:

\[ R'(x)-2xR(x)=1 \]

No rational function satisfies this equation. Therefore, the antiderivative of e−x² cannot be expressed using elementary functions. The same conclusion applies to the normal CDF after a change of variable.

This does not mean that the normal CDF cannot be represented analytically. It means that its expression requires a special function rather than elementary functions alone.

The error function

The error function, erf, is introduced precisely to represent integrals involving e−x². It is defined by:

\[ \operatorname{erf}(z) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^z e^{-t^2} \, dt \]

Consequently, the standard normal CDF has the exact analytical representation:

\[ \Phi(x)= \frac{1}{2} \left[ 1+\operatorname{erf} \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \right] \]

The error function provides an exact mathematical representation, but its values still need to be evaluated numerically in practical applications. Numerical algorithms are therefore essential for calculating probabilities efficiently and accurately.

Conclusions

The numerical experiments show that the standard normal CDF can be evaluated using several different approaches. Although these approaches are based on different mathematical ideas, they should produce results close to the same theoretical probability.

The trapezoidal rule provides a direct numerical approximation of the integral. Its accuracy generally improves as the number of subdivisions increases, but this also increases computational cost.

The power series provides another way to calculate the CDF. It avoids numerical integration and converges to the exact error function representation. However, for large absolute values of x, its terms can become very large before cancellation occurs, which may reduce numerical precision.

The error function approximation is particularly efficient because it evaluates the CDF using a fixed number of arithmetic operations. It is therefore suitable for applications that require many CDF evaluations. Its disadvantage is that it introduces a small approximation error.

The comparison with the NIST reference values allows us to evaluate the accuracy of the implementations. It also illustrates that numerical algorithms must balance accuracy, speed, and numerical stability.

Finally, Liouville's theorem explains why the Gaussian integral cannot be expressed in elementary functions. The introduction of the error function solves the problem of analytical representation, while numerical algorithms make the function practically computable.

Overall, the best method depends on the application: numerical integration is intuitive and flexible, power series are useful for understanding the mathematical structure, and error-function approximations are efficient for practical computation.
