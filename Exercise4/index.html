Ex.1 Welford's Online Algorithm

Welford's algorithm computes the mean and variance of a sequence incrementally, without storing all previous observations. It is based on the same recurrence approach used by Knuth for the online computation of the mean.

Recurrence for the Mean

Suppose we have already processed n observations: x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ. Their mean is:
μₙ = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) / n
When a new observation xₙ₊₁ arrives, the new mean is:
μₙ₊₁ = μₙ + (xₙ₊₁ - μₙ) / (n + 1)
Define the difference:
δ = xₙ₊₁ - μₙ
Then:
μₙ₊₁ = μₙ + δ / (n + 1)

Derivation of the Sum of Squares (SS)

Define the sum of squared deviations from the current mean:
SSₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ - μₙ)²
After adding a new observation, we have:
SSₙ₊₁ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ⁺¹ (xᵢ - μₙ₊₁)²
Separate the previous observations from the new one:
SSₙ₊₁ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ - μₙ₊₁)² + (xₙ₊₁ - μₙ₊₁)²
Since:
xᵢ - μₙ₊₁ = (xᵢ - μₙ) - (μₙ₊₁ - μₙ)
Expanding the square and using:
Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ - μₙ) = 0
gives:
SSₙ₊₁ = SSₙ + n(μₙ₊₁ - μₙ)² + (xₙ₊₁ - μₙ₊₁)²
Substituting the mean recurrence:
μₙ₊₁ - μₙ = δ/(n+1)
and:
xₙ₊₁ - μₙ₊₁ = nδ/(n+1)
we obtain:
SSₙ₊₁ = SSₙ + δ² n/(n+1)
Equivalently, since δ = xₙ₊₁ - μₙ:
SSₙ₊₁ = SSₙ + δ(xₙ₊₁ - μₙ₊₁)

Derivation of the Variance

The population variance of the n observations is:
σ²ₙ = SSₙ / n
The unbiased sample variance is:
s²ₙ = SSₙ / (n - 1),   n > 1
Therefore, once SS is updated, variance can be calculated directly without storing the observations.

The distinction is important: dividing by n gives the population variance of the observed dataset, while dividing by n - 1 gives the usual unbiased sample variance estimator.

Welford's Algorithm — Implementation

The recurrence can be implemented with just three core update operations, maintaining only the count, mean, and sum of squared deviations.

Welford's recurrence 
// Update the running statistics
count++;

const delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / count;
SS += delta * (x - mean);

After processing all observations, calculate the variance:

Variance calculation 
const populationVariance = SS / count;
const sampleVariance =
    count > 1 ? SS / (count - 1) : NaN;

Complexity: O(1) time per observation and O(1) additional memory. No previous observations need to be stored.

Conclusions

1. Convergence of the sampling mean

The first important observation is that the average of the sampling means calculated across all hackers tends to approach the theoretical mean of the probability distribution, μ = 5. Each hacker obtains a different sample of scores, so their individual sampling means will generally be different. However, when we consider a sufficiently large number of hackers, these differences tend to balance out.

This behavior is explained by the Law of Large Numbers: as the number of independent observations increases, the sample mean becomes a more reliable estimate of the population mean. In our experiment, increasing m makes the average of the hackers' sampling means more stable, while increasing n makes each individual hacker's estimate more accurate.

2. Convergence of the sampling variance

A similar behavior can be observed for the sampling variance. The average of the variances calculated by the hackers tends to approach the theoretical variance σ² = 10. This indicates that the sample variance can be used to estimate the variability of the underlying probability distribution.

An important detail is that we calculate each sample variance using n − 1 rather than n as the denominator. This correction is known as Bessel's correction and makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the population variance. If we divided by n instead, the estimates would systematically underestimate the theoretical variance.

3. Effect of increasing the number of systems

When we increase n, each hacker collects more observations. Consequently, their sampling mean and sampling variance become less sensitive to individual random scores. This can be observed in the simulation by looking at the standard deviations of the sampling means and sampling variances across hackers.

In particular, the standard deviation of the sampling means decreases proportionally to 1/√n. This means that doubling the number of systems does not halve the variability of the sampling means; instead, it reduces it by a factor of √2.

The sampling variances also become more concentrated around the theoretical variance as n increases. Therefore, larger samples provide more consistent estimates of both the mean and the variance.

4. Importance in inferential statistics

These experiments illustrate two fundamental ideas in inferential statistics: estimation and uncertainty. In practical situations, the true probability distribution of a population is usually unknown. We cannot observe every possible outcome, so we collect a sample and use statistics calculated from that sample to estimate unknown population parameters.

The sampling mean provides an estimate of the population mean, while the sampling variance provides an estimate of the population variance. By repeating the experiment with different samples, we can study how much these estimates vary and how reliable they are likely to be.

This is particularly important when constructing confidence intervals and performing hypothesis tests. These procedures depend on understanding the sampling distributions of estimators and how their uncertainty changes with the sample size.

5. Role of Welford's algorithm

Finally, Welford's recurrence formulas allow us to calculate the sample mean and variance incrementally, without storing all the observations or repeatedly processing the entire sample. This is particularly useful when dealing with large datasets or continuous streams of observations.

Moreover, Welford's algorithm is numerically stable because it avoids directly subtracting two potentially large quantities, as happens in some traditional variance formulas. Therefore, it is an efficient and reliable method for computing statistical estimates in simulations and real-world applications.

Overall conclusion

The simulation confirms that sample statistics are useful estimators of population parameters. Increasing the number of hackers improves the stability of the averages across independent experiments, while increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker reduces the variability of the individual estimates. Together, these observations demonstrate why collecting sufficiently large samples is essential for drawing reliable statistical conclusions.
